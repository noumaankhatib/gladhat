## True Stories

Listen carefully.

Connect the dots.

### Seeing Through the Buyer's Eyes
*A true story from my work with Server Factory*

When I first started working with Server Factory, the conversation wasn't about psychology. It wasn't about positioning. It wasn't even about websites.
Like many growing businesses, they wanted more enquiries, more opportunities and more sales.
The obvious answer seemed to be a new website, Google Ads, connected LinkedIn, and better-designed trade show booths.
Perhaps a few improvements to the messaging.
Before making any recommendations, I wanted to understand something much more important.
I needed to understand how organisations, from universities to high-frequency trading companies, actually choose a server supplier.

**Starting with curiosity**

I began by spending time understanding the business from different perspectives.
I interviewed members of the team.
I created a customer questionnaire, and Server Factory sent it out to existing clients. A significant number of clients took the time to respond.
I was interested what mattered most to them.
What created confidence?
Where were the frustrations?
What did customers value once the purchase had been made?
The answers were sometimes surprising.
And they began to challenge assumptions inside the business itself.
Different people saw different strengths.
Customers valued things the team hadn't always fully appreciated.
Next, I analysed the key messages of competitors across the market.
I wasn't looking for clever marketing ideas. I was looking for patterns in what competitors choose to say about themselves. How they approach their customers. How they build trust.
Little by little, the picture became clearer.

**Looking through the buyer's eyes**

As I explored the online market, one thing became impossible to ignore.
A proportion of competitors were selling servers. They offered what amounted to a catalogue.
In these cases, the burden of making the right decision sat almost entirely with the customer.
Only a few pushed consultancy and seemed to actively simplify the decision making process.
Research showed there were two types of customer - the one who is an expert and knows what he wants, and the one who isn't sure and needs advice. I knew we had to differentiate both of these user flows. We needed to provide help in as clear and simple a way as possible.
And so, instead of merely asking:
"How do we sell more servers?"
We began focusing on customers who needed advice, asking:
"How do we help organisations make better buying decisions?"
Everything that followed grew from that shift in perspective.

**Building confidence before selling**

The website wasn't simply redesigned.
It was reorganised around the ways the two different types of customers actually think.
Trust signals became more prominent.
Buying guides were introduced to help simplify complex decisions.
The goal was to reduce uncertainty.

**From website to commercial strategy**

Something interesting happened along the way.
The project stopped being about a website.
The research influenced Google Ads.
It influenced positioning.
It shaped LinkedIn content.
It inspired ideas for educational resources.
None of those ideas appeared in the original brief.
They emerged because we kept following the questions.
Every answer uncovered another opportunity.

**The results**

The website became much more than a marketing asset. It became a tool designed to support buying decisions, where an SEO and AEO strategy was implemented before copywriting began. Organic leads have grown exponentially.

The website project led on to a three-month pilot Google Ads campaign. A media spend of $7500 led to more than 50 qualified leads, and more than $1.3 million in sales.

As well as the ROI, Server Factory developed a much clearer understanding of what made it different. It is far more than simply the hardware it supplies. It is the confidence, expertise and guidance it gives customers when they make important technology decisions.

**What this project taught me**

Before better marketing, businesses need a clearer understanding of how their customers make decisions.
Once you understand that, the messaging becomes clearer.
The website becomes easier to structure. Advertising becomes more effective. New opportunities begin to appear.
This project reminded me that the most valuable work often happens long before a headline is written or a campaign goes live.
It begins by asking better questions, by taking a step back and seeing the business through different eyes.

### Finding the Right Language
*A true story from my work with First Light*

Professor Amit Zoran came to me with something more valuable than a conventional brief: a distinctive vision.
It brought together technology, nature, education and wellbeing in a way I had not encountered before. Instead of treating technology as something that inevitably distracts us from ourselves, Amit believed it could help us become more present, more aware and more connected with the natural world.
The challenge was not creating the idea.
It was finding language that allowed other people to understand and feel it.

**Beginning with the idea**

From a communications perspective, we needed to understand what problems Amit's method was targeting.
What are the problems caused by our daily use of tech?
How are people already looking for help?
Where is Amit's method different?
What should someone feel after encountering Amit's website and social media?
These were the first questions.
The more we explored it together, this was a translation project as much as a branding project. In the sense that an amazing vision already existed. Our job was to translate it into language that other people could immediately understand.

**Looking beyond the obvious**

As I explored the wider landscape, I noticed something interesting.
Many conversations about technology and wellbeing seemed to force people into choosing between two opposing ideas.
Technology was either the problem.
Or it was the solution.
Neither reflected Amit's thinking.
His vision wasn't about escaping technology. Nor was it about celebrating technology for its own sake. It was about using technology thoughtfully, as a way of helping people reconnect with themselves, with nature and with the present moment.
That distinction changed everything.
It wasn't simply a positioning statement.
It became the philosophy that shaped every decision that followed.

**Finding the right language**

Once we understood the essence of the project, the creative work became surprisingly natural.
The name First Light emerged because it captured exactly what the project represented.
The quiet beginning of awareness. The first moment of seeing differently. The start of a journey rather than its destination.
The tagline followed.
Come Home.
Not to a building. Not to a place. To yourself. To the feeling of being alive that connects all beings.
Alongside the name and tagline came the wider brand story, the messaging, the website and the launch communications.
The ideas that emerged reflected something that was already there.
We weren't inventing an identity. We were uncovering one.

**From brand to experience**

The website didn't need to explain everything. It needed to invite people into the pathways Amit had uncovered.
In its clarity, the messaging was calm and simple. It spoke to real needs.
Amit's visual identity beautifully supported his philosophy. Every piece of communication became another expression of the same central idea.
We can't realistically return to nature by getting rid of technology; we can use technology to rediscover nature and dance with both.

**What this project taught me**

Working with Amit was unusual and rewarding. He moves easily between design, software, philosophy and research, producing ideas faster than most people could absorb them.
The challenge was not generating possibilities. It was deciding which ideas would help an unfamiliar audience understand First Light and recognise why it mattered.
Research gave us the filter. Once the picture became clear, the name, messages and design direction became simpler. Everything began to work together.

### Recognising the Value of Thinking
*A true story from my work with Tonbo Ventures*

Tonbo Ventures initially asked for help communicating its venture studio on LinkedIn.
The brief covered positioning, messaging, copywriting and design.
But the content immediately raised broader questions.
How did founders view investors?
How did LPs understand venture studios in comparison with venture capital funds?
How could Tonbo distinguish itself in the climate-tech investment market?
The conversations needed to answer those questions became part of the work.

**The invisible part of the project**

Like many people working in communications, I had fallen into a common trap.
I had been hired to produce deliverables.
The copy. The messaging. A deck upgrade. The Naming of a Venture Studio Program.

All these were visible outputs, but they weren't the most important work.
That occurred during conversations. When assumptions were questioned. When ideas connected. When a different way of looking at the business began to emerge. The marketing materials were the visible results of deeper thinking. My value lay in the thinking. I hadn't fully recognised that yet.

**An uncomfortable lesson**

As the project came to an end, we disagreed about the value of the work that had evolved throughout the engagement.
At the time, it was frustrating.
Looking back, I no longer see it simply as a disagreement over payment.
I see it as evidence that I hadn't yet become clear about the nature and real value of my own work.
I had helped define the value of another business.
I hadn't yet defined the value of my own.
That realisation stayed with me long after the project had finished.

**Changing how I work**

The experience prompted me to start rethinking how I describe what I do.
I had realised clients weren't only asking me to write.
They were asking me to envisage, to filter, to challenge assumptions, connect ideas, notice opportunities that weren't immediately obvious, and help them see their business from a different perspective.
The writing mattered.
But it was only one expression of the thinking behind it.
That understanding has led me to change the way I structure projects, the conversations I have with clients, and the way I value strategic work.

**What this project taught me**

Some valuable work leaves little behind. A conversation, a question, a shift in perspective, a decision made with greater clarity. Those moments don't always appear in proposals or a list of deliverables.
They're difficult to measure, and even more difficult to price.
But they often create the greatest commercial value.
This project reminded me that thinking isn't something that happens before my work begins.
Sometimes it is my work.
Recognising that has made me a better consultant, a clearer communicator and, I hope, a better partner for the founders I work with today.

### Connecting Expertise with Understanding
*A true story from my work with enSights*

When I first started working with enSights, the technology was not unique, but it was certainly ahead of the curve.
enSights is a SaaS platform that helps organisations operating solar assets improve performance, identify faults and make better operational decisions.

The startup was already doing business and had a growing list of clients. The communications challenge was helping people understand why enSights mattered.
The team understood their product inside out. Explaining its multiple benefits to potential customers in written and visual communications was harder.
Like many software companies, the language naturally drifted towards features and capabilities.
The more interesting question was different.
How do you break through the noise to communicate with the various types of people who are responsible for investing in, building and operating renewable energy sites?

**Speaking the customer's language**

We explored existing messaging. Not because it was poor, but because it did not always reflect the conversations customers were actually having.
We discussed positioning.
Taglines.
The fundamental problems the business solved.
The language people used inside the industry.
Slowly, the focus shifted away from describing software features, towards looking at the daily challenges faced by asset owners and operators.

**The best work happened in conversation**

A key part of the project was positioning enSights as industry insiders who understand the concerns of renewable energy asset owners and operators.

The chosen method was LinkedIn articles. The content was semi-technical.
Clearly, I'm no engineer. I wasn't hired because I understood every technical detail. The articles worked because they were written by me, collaborating with their business development director. We'd choose a technical topic. He'd explain it from the perspective of someone with years of industry experience. I'd keep asking questions. Why does that matter? What problem does it solve? Why should an operator care?
By the end of the conversation, I understood enough to write for the audience.
The articles became clearer because they reflected both market know-how, technical expertise and curiosity.

**Beyond content**

As the relationship developed, the work expanded.
I interviewed customers and created videos.
I supported business development by reaching out to organisations operating solar assets, helping generate meetings that opened new commercial conversations.
None of those activities had been part of a grand plan.
They emerged because we kept asking the same question.
What would help the business communicate more clearly with the customers it wanted to reach?

**What this project taught me**

When someone is willing to keep asking, "Why does that matter?" and someone technical is willing to keep explaining, complicated subjects become easier to understand. This way, businesses begin to communicate more naturally and expertise becomes translatable.
Sometimes my role isn't just to provide the answers; it's first to create the conditions in which better answers can emerge.

### Simplifying Complexity
*A true story from my work with Provengo*

Instead of forcing engineers to describe exactly what a system should do, Provengo allowed companies to specify what they did not want, dramatically simplifying the design of complex systems.
It sounds simple. It is not. This was one of the most technically sophisticated products I had encountered.

**Brilliant technology isn't enough**

The founders were computer science professors from Ben-Gurion University.
Their expertise was extraordinary. They also knew something needed sharpening in the way they explained Provengo - the implications were so big for systems engineering, they needed to really get people's attention.
They had a number of super impressive clients, early adopters, who gave glowing references. Yet in their conversations with investors and potential new customers, they often found themselves struggling with the same problem.
There were countless ways of explaining their new technology.
Each member of the team had a slightly different idea about where the story should begin. Every explanation was technically accurate. Few explanations helped people adequately grasp the significance.
The real challenge wasn't building the website; it was creating clarity.

**Looking through the audience's eyes**

One advantage of not being a systems engineer is that I naturally asked some of the same questions an investor would ask.
What does that actually mean?
Why is that important?
How will this change the way organisations work?
Instead of trying to explain every aspect of the technology, we focused on helping people understand the problem it solved.
We reduced the reliance on technical terms. Each sentence carried one clear idea.
Instead of asking visitors to understand the entire platform, we gave them enough clarity to understand why they should keep reading, or book a demo.
That shift influenced everything that followed.

**Finding the essence**

As the messaging became clearer, another piece fell into place.
The tagline.
Make Systems Smarter.
Three simple words.
They weren't intended to explain the platform. They were intended to express its purpose. Everything else on the website grew naturally from that idea.
Instead of beginning with technology, the conversation began with people.
The challenges they faced. The decisions they made. The outcomes they wanted. Only then did the platform itself take centre stage.
The website wasn't trying to impress visitors with complexity. It was helping them understand why the technology mattered.

**Building the bridge**

Working on Provengo reminded me that my role isn't to try to become the technical expert.
The founders already had that covered.
My role was to stand between deep expertise and the people it needed to reach.
To ask the questions that a customer might ask.
To notice where explanations became too complicated.
To keep searching for the simplest expression of a sophisticated idea without losing what made it valuable.
Looking back, I think that's what the project was really about.
Not reducing complexity.
Building a bridge across it.

**What this project taught me**

It's easy to assume that the answer to a complicated product is a more detailed explanation.
I've found the opposite is often true. People don't need to understand everything before their interest is sparked and they begin to care. They need to understand enough to recognise why something matters. Once that happens, curiosity takes over. The deeper conversations can come later.
This project reminded me that clarity isn't about making ideas smaller. It's about making them accessible. Sometimes the greatest value isn't explaining more. It's helping people take the first confident step towards understanding.

## See Your Business Differently

As a founder, you already know your products, industry and customers better than almost anyone.
What you may need is a different perspective.
The questions below aren't a test. There are no right answers. They're simply designed to help you step outside your business for a few minutes and see it through fresh eyes.
You may discover nothing new. Or you may find yourself asking a completely different question.
Either outcome is valuable.

**Before you begin...**

I'd like to ask one small favour.
Please grab a notebook and a pen.
You could type your answers into your phone or laptop.
But I think you'll get much more from this if you write them by hand.
There's no rush.
These aren't test questions.
There are no scores.
No right answers.
They're simply an invitation to step outside your business for a little while and see it through fresh eyes.
Some questions may take thirty seconds.
Others may stay with you for a good while.
That's perfectly okay. Take your time.
When you're ready...
Let's begin.

**1. What would your best customers say you do better than anyone else?**
Not what you believe.
Not what your website says.
What would they say?
Write down the first three answers that come to mind.
Then ask yourself:
How much of my marketing actually focuses on those strengths?

**2. Imagine today is your first day in the business.**
You know nothing.
You've just arrived.
You visit your website.
You read your brochure.
You browse your LinkedIn page.
What would confuse you?
What assumptions are you expected to understand?
What questions remain unanswered?

**3. If your business disappeared tomorrow...**
What would your customers genuinely miss?
Not the products.
Not the features.
What difference would disappear from their lives or businesses?

**4. If you could communicate only one thing...**
Imagine you could leave every visitor with just one clear understanding of your business.
What would it be?
Would your website, presentations and sales conversations all communicate that same idea?
If not, why not?

**5. What assumptions have you stopped questioning?**
Every business develops habits.
Messages become familiar.
Processes become fixed.
Ideas become accepted simply because they've existed for a long time.
Ask yourself:
What if one of our biggest assumptions isn't true anymore?

**6. Where might the biggest opportunity be hiding?**
Not the opportunity you're already pursuing.
The one you haven't considered.
A different audience.
A new partnership.
A simpler offer.
A completely different way of explaining what you do.
Or perhaps something else entirely.
If you gave yourself permission to look beyond the obvious...
What might you discover?

**A final thought**

These questions aren't designed to give you answers.
They're designed to help you ask better questions.
In my experience, that's where the most valuable commercial conversations begin.
If one of these exercises has helped you see your business differently, I'd love to hear what you discovered.

Or - Close your notebook for a moment.
What's the one thought that's stayed with you?

## Who am I?

People sometimes ask me what I do.
The honest answer is that it varies from project to project.
Sometimes I'm researching customers. Sometimes I'm simplifying complicated ideas. Sometimes I'm helping founders find the right language for something they've been struggling to explain.
The work looks a bit different every time. The purpose is always the same.
I help businesses see themselves more clearly.

**It took me a long time to realise…**

For years, I described myself as a copywriter. Then a marketing consultant. Both descriptions were partly true. Neither felt complete.
Looking back, I can see that the writing was secondary. It was the outcome.
The real work happened earlier. It was the listening, the researching, the asking questions, the looking through the customer's eyes, the intuition to put my finger where it mattered.
The websites, presentations, campaigns and messaging all came afterwards.

**Curiosity has always been my favourite tool**

I've worked with businesses in AI infrastructure, renewable energy, software, venture capital, wellbeing, education and even cosmetics.
On the surface, they couldn't be more different. What connects them isn't the industry. It's curiosity. Every project begins with the same belief.
Before deciding what to say, it's worth digging for what really needs to be said.
That usually means asking questions, sometimes uncomfortable ones, sometimes obvious ones that nobody has asked for a long time.
Sometimes the answer changes everything.

**The moments I enjoy most**

People often assume my favourite part of a project is writing.
It isn't.
It's when someone suddenly stops and says,
"Yes, that's it."
"That's what we've been trying to say."
Or...
"I've never looked at it like that before."
Once the thinking becomes clear, everything else tends to follow naturally.

**What I've learned**

The projects I've worked on have taught me:
To see through the buyer's eyes, and to find the right language.
Expertise, however brilliant, has to be understandable.
Clear agreements protect good relationships.
Every project has left me seeing business a little differently than before.

**Outside work**

When I'm not working, you'll usually find me somewhere quieter.
Walking in nature, practising yoga, meditating, playing music, or spending time with my son. Those things aren't separate from my work. They are the way I remind myself to slow down, to notice, to listen and stay curious.

**If any of this resonates...**

Perhaps we should have a conversation.
Together, we may uncover a few interesting questions.

## Ready for a chat?

If you've made it this far, thank you.

### Implementation notes

- Use one clear H1 per page. The H1 does not have to match the SEO title word for word, but both should describe the same subject.
- Write the title and meta description directly in WordPress rather than injecting them with JavaScript.
- Use descriptive internal link text. Prefer "Read the Server Factory story" to generic labels such as "Click here".
- Add meaningful alt text to portfolio images. Describe the work and its context, not decorative details.
- Keep written content available as normal page text, even where audio, video or visual effects are added.

### SEO reference note

The SEO recommendations follow current Google Search Central guidance: unique and descriptive page titles, useful page-specific descriptions, readable URLs, accessible text and descriptive internal links. Google does not use the meta-keywords tag for ranking.
